Advanced: what the sight scale was regulated for, and fitting

The ladder's marks are modelled as the elevation a Mk VII-type service round needs at that range. Your firings then correct that model for your own rifle and load.

Pressure is the actual (station) reading at the range, not the sea-level figure from a forecast. Velocity from the box is usually from a longer test barrel; a chronograph figure is better.

For each group, enter where its centre landed relative to your point of aim: + is high, − is low. In Sight set, put the mark (300) or B for the battle aperture, and any clicks above or below the mark in Clicks ± (e.g. +2 or -3, Mk1 only). Typing 500+2 straight into Sight set also works. Shots weights each group in the fit. Untick Use to leave out a flyer or a bad day.

Aim: Point of impact means you aimed at the spot you wanted to hit. Lollipop means you held the bottom of the chosen target (or of the round bull's black aiming mark) on top of the foresight. Either way, measure Up/down and Left/right from where you wanted the group to land: the middle of the scoring circles. The tool allows for the hold-off using the target sizes in the Lollipop section. Round-bull faces exist for 200 to 600 yd and long range (900/1000); other ranges are left out.

Units apply to up/down, left/right and group size: in = inches on the target at that range; MOA = minutes of angle; ZR card in = inches measured on the Bisley Zero Range card (fired at 71′ 7″, where ¼ in ≈ 1 MOA). Under each group size the tool shows what it equals in MOA and in inches at the range it represents. For Zero Range groups, put the card mark you aimed at (e.g. 300) as the range and measure from that mark. The card is laid out for a standard trajectory rather than your exact load, so confirm long-range settings on the long range.

Lollipop settings

Settings for a 6 o'clock hold: the bottom of the target (or of the black aiming mark on a round bull) sits on top of the foresight, and the sight is set so the group centre lands on the middle of the scoring circles. Each cell shows the setting and how far the group centre should land from that middle (+ high). Ranges come from the Sight settings table above.

Target dimensions used

From the 2026 NRA Handbook (Bisley Bible), Appendix V: Fig 12C is 400 mm high with its circle centred 200 mm below the top; Fig 12 is the top half of a Fig 11 with its circle centred 300 mm below the top; round-bull aiming marks are 406 mm (200 yd), 560 mm (300), 813 mm (400, not used at Bisley), 990 mm (500/600) and 1120 mm (long range, used here for 900 and 1000). The Fig 11 circle centre (506 mm below the top) is from the NRA CSR Handbook. The Handbook doesn't give the Fig 11 height, so 1140 mm is from MoD range design guidance, and the Fig 12 height (570 mm) is half of that. Change any of these if your targets differ. Historic round bulls only have 200, 300 and 500/600 yd faces.

Trajectory

Height of the bullet above (+) or below (−) your line of sight, in inches, for the sight setting chosen. Tap or hover on the plot to read off any range. The sight's elevation comes from your calibration, so a different load is shown as if fired with the same sight setting on this rifle. Treat comparisons between loads as a guide until you have shot them.

How it works

A point-mass trajectory (G1 drag, your temperature and pressure) gives the elevation your load needs at every range. Each ladder mark is treated as the elevation a Mk VII-type round needs at that distance, which is how the scale was laid out.

Each test group tells the tool how much elevation that setting actually gave on your rifle: the elevation needed plus how high the group was. With groups at one setting it fits a fixed offset (in effect, foresight height). With groups at two or more settings 200+ yards apart it also fits a scale factor, which absorbs differences in velocity and BC from the reference round. Residuals show how well the model explains each group; large ones point to a flyer, a typo or changed conditions.

Treat predictions beyond your furthest test range as a starting point and confirm on paper. Rows in orange are where the bullet is near the speed of sound and drops off quickly, so they are less certain.
